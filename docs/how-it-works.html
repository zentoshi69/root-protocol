HoodPups · how settlement works

Moving Robinhood money back to Bitcoin owners

The protocol does not move Bitcoin Puppets across chains. It moves payment in the other direction — and the Puppet never leaves the wallet it started in.

The problem, stated exactly

Alice, on Robinhood Chain, wants a HoodPup derived from Bitcoin Puppet #123. Bob owns that Puppet on Bitcoin, in a cold wallet, and has no intention of moving it, bridging it, or handing it to a custodian.

A Robinhood Chain contract cannot read Bitcoin. It cannot parse an Ordinals index, inspect a UTXO set, or verify a signature over a Taproot script. So the protocol doesn't pretend it can. Instead it splits the problem into three machines, and only one thing ever crosses between them.

Bob picks how he gets paid

Ownership being proven is the same in both cases. What differs is what happens next, and the ordering on the Bitcoin path is the part worth reading twice.

What the quorum can and cannot do

This is the honest part, and it is the first thing in the repository’s documentation rather than a footnote. Three colluding operators can lie about Bitcoin. Here is exactly how far that goes.

A dishonest 3-of-5 quorumWhat that means
can assert a false ownership factA HoodPup mints to the wrong recipient and the seller share is credited to the wrong address.
can assert a false payment factA solver is reimbursed from escrow for a Bitcoin payment it never made.
can censorRefuse to attest anything. Escrowed funds are still refundable at expiry, so this costs time, not principal.
cannot move anyone’s Bitcoin PuppetStructural, not policy. The protocol holds no Bitcoin key, receives no seed, and never requires the inscription to move.
cannot take credited balancesThere is no code path that reduces a user’s claimable balance. Not for an admin, not for anyone.
cannot mint a second HoodPup for a RootPermanent and uncleanable, including by the deployer.
cannot reuse a Bitcoin paymentEach txid:vout is consumed globally, once.
cannot change the 50 / 25 / 25 splitCompiled into the bytecode with no setter and no upgrade path.
cannot redirect a seller’s payoutThe escrow pays the address inside the signed message and no other.

That asymmetry is the whole design. The worst case is a bad mint and a bad reimbursement — not a stolen Bitcoin asset. Anything in this project that reads as “trustless bridge” or “atomic swap” is wrong, and a test fails the build if either phrase reaches user-facing copy.

Where the money goes

Every paid mint splits the same way, computed from the gross escrow. Seller and treasury are floor-divided and the protocol absorbs the remainder, so the three parts sum to the gross exactly — including at one, two and three wei, where three independent divisions would strand dust forever.

50% Bitcoin Puppet controller

Paid as ETH on Robinhood Chain, or as exact native BTC through a bonded solver.

25% Puppet ecosystem treasury

Destination is governable through the timelock. The percentage is not.

25% Protocol

Absorbs the rounding remainder, which is what makes conservation exact.

What happens when Bob sells the Puppet

Paying a previous holder forever after they sold would be a bug, so ownership is tracked as epochs. A watcher sees the inscription’s UTXO get spent and submits a spend attestation; the Root goes inactive and future value accrues to a pending bucket rather than to Bob. Charlie proves control, a new epoch opens, and the pending balance releases to him.

Money Bob already earned stays Bob’s. Only future value follows the new owner. The registry records attested state rather than live Bitcoin state, so there is a real window between the sale and the attestation — value accrued in it is recoverable because it lands in the pending bucket, and the window is disclosed rather than hidden.
